# Plain-language writing style

Write in plain, direct English. Help the reader understand the information on the first reading.

This guide has three parts:

1. Core rules for all writing
2. Technical and procedural writing
3. Editorial and journalistic writing

Apply the core rules to every document. Then apply the rules for the document's purpose.

## Choose the writing mode

Use **technical and procedural writing** when the reader must:

- Complete a task
- Operate, configure, maintain, or troubleshoot something
- Understand how a product, process, or system works
- Follow requirements, limits, or safety controls

Use **editorial and journalistic writing** when the reader must:

- Understand what happened or what is changing
- Evaluate claims, evidence, significance, or uncertainty
- Understand a reported event, argument, analysis, or opinion
- Know how the information was established

A document can use both modes. Apply the appropriate mode to each section. For example, use editorial rules to explain why a product changed and technical rules to provide its migration steps.

When rules conflict, use the rule that is more specific to the content. Safety, legal, regulatory, accessibility, and publication requirements take precedence over this guide. Approved project terminology takes precedence over general word preferences.

# Core rules for all writing

## Purpose and audience

- Identify what the reader needs to know or do.
- Give the reader the information needed for that purpose.
- Do not assume specialist knowledge unless the audience is defined as specialist.
- Explain why information matters when its relevance is not self-evident.
- Omit material that does not help the reader understand, decide, or act.

## Register and tone

- Use a clear, factual, and professional tone.
- Be direct without being abrupt.
- Sound natural, not bureaucratic, academic, promotional, or mechanical.
- Prefer precision to drama.
- Do not exaggerate, sensationalize, or manufacture urgency.
- Avoid clichés, slogans, stock phrases, slang, and decorative metaphors.
- Do not describe something as `obvious`, `clear`, or `undeniable` in place of explaining it.
- Do not use loaded language to imply a conclusion that the evidence does not support.
- Use gender-neutral and inclusive language.
- Use American English spelling unless the project or publication uses another standard.

## Vocabulary and terminology

- Prefer common, familiar words.
- Use concrete words with precise meanings.
- Prefer the shortest word that preserves the full meaning.
- Use the same term for the same defined person, component, action, event, or concept.
- Do not replace a defined term with a synonym merely to add variety.
- Use approved terminology when it exists.
- Use a specialized term only when it is necessary and accurate.
- Explain an unfamiliar term when it first appears.
- Write a name or term in full on first reference. Then use an approved abbreviation or a clear short form.
- Do not make the reader decode an abbreviation.
- Avoid jargon, business language, legal language, and academic language when a common word gives the same meaning.
- Avoid euphemisms that hide responsibility, harm, cost, or consequence.
- Prefer strong verbs to abstract nouns.
- Avoid noun-heavy phrases and fashionable conversions of nouns into verbs.
- Use words in their natural grammatical form unless the subject has established a specialized use.
- Avoid Latin abbreviations in prose. Write `for example`, `that is`, or `and so on` when needed.

Choose the clearest word in context. Common preferences include:

- `use` instead of `utilize`
- `start` instead of `commence`
- `help` instead of `facilitate`
- `before` instead of `prior to`
- `because` instead of `due to the fact that`
- `try` instead of `attempt` when used as a verb
- `now` instead of `at this point in time`
- `most` instead of `the majority of`

These examples are preferences, not automatic substitutions. Rewrite the complete sentence when a word replacement makes it awkward or changes its meaning.

## Requirements and possibility

Use modal verbs consistently:

- Use `must` for a requirement.
- Use `should` for a recommendation.
- Use `can` for capability or ordinary permission.
- Use `may` only when its meaning is clear or when a governing standard requires it.
- Do not use `shall` unless a legal or regulatory convention requires it.

## Sentences and voice

- Write short sentences.
- Put one main idea in each sentence.
- Aim for 25 words or fewer in general prose.
- Rewrite a sentence longer than 30 words unless it remains immediately clear.
- Put the most important information near the start.
- Keep the subject and verb close together.
- Use an action verb to describe an action.
- Do not hide an action inside an abstract noun phrase.
- Prefer active voice when the actor is known and relevant.
- Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or less important than the result.
- Name the actor when responsibility matters.
- Use connecting words to show cause, contrast, sequence, and result.
- Do not omit necessary words or context merely to shorten a sentence.
- Vary sentence length enough to keep prose natural, but do not sacrifice clarity for rhythm.

## Clarity and ambiguity

- Write each sentence so that it has one reasonable meaning in context.
- Put a condition before the action or result that depends on it.
- Put words and phrases next to the text they modify.
- Replace a pronoun when it can refer to more than one person or thing.
- Use `this`, `that`, `it`, and `they` only when the referent is clear.
- Repeat a noun when repetition prevents confusion.
- Make the relationship between an action and its cause explicit.
- State the relevant place, date, period, unit, comparison, limit, direction, location, and sequence.
- Avoid relative terms such as `recently`, `soon`, `many`, or `significant` without enough context.
- Identify the basis of a comparison. Do not write `higher`, `lower`, `better`, or `worse` without saying compared with what.
- Review sentences that use `with`. Make sure the word expresses one clear relationship.

## Facts, claims, and certainty

- Distinguish established facts from reported claims, estimates, predictions, analysis, inference, and opinion.
- State who knows, believes, estimates, expects, or disputes something when that distinction matters.
- Do not imply more certainty than the available evidence supports.
- Identify information that is unknown, unverified, disputed, estimated, or subject to change.
- Put attribution close to the statement it supports.
- Distinguish correlation from established cause.

## Structure

- Put the central information first.
- Give necessary context before details that depend on it.
- Introduce one new idea at a time.
- Keep one topic in each paragraph.
- Start a new paragraph when the subject, speaker, time, place, or line of thought changes.
- Use informative headings in longer documents.
- Use a vertical list when readers must compare several parallel items or follow a sequence.
- Make list items grammatically parallel.
- Do not repeat information merely to add emphasis or length.
- End when the reader has the information needed to understand or act.

## Numbers, dates, and comparisons

- Use exact numbers when the source supports exactness.
- Round numbers when added precision would mislead or distract.
- Identify estimates as estimates.
- Give a source and date for statistics that can change.
- State units and use the same unit when comparing values.
- Include the denominator when reporting a rate or percentage.
- Give both a percentage and its underlying count when either number alone could mislead.
- Distinguish percentage change from percentage-point change.
- Use an absolute date when a relative date could become unclear.
- Give a useful baseline, period, scale, or comparison for numbers that readers cannot readily interpret.

## Contractions, humor, and figurative language

- Use common contractions in general and editorial prose when they suit the publication's voice.
- Avoid contractions in safety text, specifications, localization-sensitive content, and highly formal procedures.
- Do not use slang contractions.
- Do not use humor in safety text, instructions, specifications, or factual reference material.
- In editorial work, use humor only when it serves the subject and cannot obscure or distort the meaning.
- Use figurative language sparingly. Do not use it when readers could interpret it literally or when precision matters.

## Punctuation and formatting

- Use standard English punctuation.
- Prefer a period when separate sentences are easier to read.
- Use a semicolon only when it makes a close relationship clearer.
- Use parentheses sparingly for references, identifiers, abbreviations, alternatives, or brief nonessential explanations.
- Use a colon to introduce a quotation, explanation, or vertical list when the preceding text is complete.
- Avoid exclamation points in technical, news, and explanatory prose.
- Do not use punctuation to manufacture drama.
- Do not use quotation marks to imply suspicion or distance the writer from an ordinary word.
- Use hyphens only when words operate together as one unit or when the applicable style requires them.

# Technical and procedural writing

The purpose of technical writing is accurate understanding and action. The reader must be able to understand the system, complete the task, and recognize the risk without guessing.

## Technical register and terminology

- Use a neutral, restrained, and factual tone.
- State facts, actions, conditions, limits, and results without commentary.
- Do not use humor, literary language, persuasive language, or conversational asides.
- Use the terminology approved by the project, company, or subject field.
- Use a technical term only when it is necessary and accurate.
- Keep a noun group to three words or fewer when possible.
- Use a preposition or clause when it makes the relationship between nouns clearer.
- Write an official long technical name in full on first use. Then use an approved abbreviation or clear short form.
- Do not force unnatural grammar to avoid every noun used as a verb or every `-ing` form. Rewrite only when the construction is unclear or needlessly abstract.

## Technical descriptions

- Give general information before detailed information.
- Describe one component, state, behavior, or relationship at a time.
- Use simple present tense for normal behavior and established facts.
- Use simple past for completed events and test results.
- Use simple future only for an actual future event or result.
- Use repeated key terms to make relationships clear.
- State inputs, outputs, defaults, dependencies, limits, and failure conditions when relevant.
- Distinguish required behavior from typical, optional, or possible behavior.
- Do not use commands in a section whose purpose is only to describe the system.
- Keep paragraphs to six sentences or fewer when possible.

## Procedures

- Write instructions as commands.
- Start each instruction with the action unless a condition must come first.
- Put one instruction in each sentence.
- Keep an instruction to 20 words or fewer when possible.
- Do not remove necessary conditions, objects, units, or safety information to meet a word target.
- Combine actions only when they occur at the same time or form one indivisible operation.
- Put a necessary condition before the command and separate it with a comma.
- Identify the object of the action.
- State the required order explicitly.
- Use a numbered list for actions that must occur in sequence.
- Use a bulleted list for unordered options, requirements, or results.
- Keep background information outside the work step when possible.
- State the expected result when the reader must confirm success.
- State what to do after a failure when recovery is within the procedure's scope.

Write:

> If the pressure is below 20 psi, open the valve.

For actions that occur in sequence, write separate steps:

1. Disconnect the cable.
2. Remove the panel.

## Notes, cautions, and warnings

- Use a note only for supplementary information.
- Do not hide a required action in a note.
- Do not use a note as a substitute for revising unclear instructions.
- Use the established label and signal word for each level of risk.
- Distinguish risk to people from risk to equipment, data, or other property.
- Do not soften, generalize, or omit safety information.

Use this order for safety text:

1. Signal word
2. Command or condition
3. Hazard
4. Possible result

Example:

> **WARNING:** Disconnect the power supply before you remove the cover. Live terminals can cause serious injury or death.

## Technical review checklist

Before publication, confirm that:

1. The document identifies what the reader must know or do.
2. Approved terms are used consistently.
3. Every instruction names a clear action and object.
4. Conditions appear before the actions that depend on them.
5. Steps are in the correct order.
6. Units, limits, tolerances, locations, and expected results are explicit.
7. Requirements, recommendations, and optional actions are distinct.
8. Notes contain information, not hidden instructions.
9. Hazards and possible results are stated directly.
10. The reader can complete the task without guessing.

# Editorial and journalistic writing

The purpose of editorial writing is informed understanding. The reader must be able to tell what happened, how it is known, why it matters, and what remains uncertain.

## Editorial register and fairness

- Write for an interested general reader, not a specialist.
- Sound informed, direct, fair, and human.
- Be direct without sounding abrupt or mechanical.
- Attribute opinions and disputed claims to their sources.
- Do not conceal advocacy inside neutral-sounding news language.
- Do not create false balance between claims with substantially different evidentiary support.
- Represent opposing evidence accurately before responding to it.
- Use first person only when the writer's role, experience, or judgment is relevant.
- Do not treat personal experience as proof of a general claim.

## Reporting and sourcing

- Verify every factual statement that the publication presents as true.
- Use primary sources when they are available and reliable.
- Attribute information that the writer did not directly observe or independently establish.
- Name the source when possible.
- Explain why an unnamed source is credible when the reader needs that context.
- Do not use a plural attribution when there is only one source.
- Do not present a press release, allegation, forecast, or opinion as an established fact.
- Give the subject of a serious allegation a fair opportunity to respond.
- Link to or identify the underlying document, data, interview, or public record when possible.
- Correct material errors promptly and plainly.

Use `said` as the standard verb for neutral attribution. Use `claimed`, `admitted`, `insisted`, `conceded`, and similar verbs only when their added judgment is accurate, relevant, and supported.

## Leads

- Start with the most important new information.
- Tell the reader why the story matters before giving secondary detail.
- Include the relevant parts of who, what, where, when, why, and how.
- Do not force all six elements into one sentence.
- Do not open with background, scene-setting, a dictionary definition, or a rhetorical question unless the format clearly benefits from it.
- Do not withhold the central fact to create suspense in a news story.
- For an explainer or feature, state the focus or central question near the start.
- After an anecdotal opening, promptly state what the larger story is about.

Write:

> The city will close three public pools next month after maintenance costs doubled in two years.

Do not write:

> Residents who enjoy swimming may soon encounter an unfortunate change at several beloved neighborhood facilities.

## Article structure

- Organize straight news from the most important information to the least important.
- Organize an explainer around the questions readers are most likely to ask.
- Use chronology when the order of events is the clearest structure.
- Keep background shorter than the new information unless the article is an explainer.
- Use one to three sentences in most paragraphs.
- Use informative subheadings in longer articles.
- End with the necessary fact, consequence, or forward-looking context. Do not add a ceremonial conclusion.

## Context and significance

- Explain why a fact matters. Do not assume the reader already knows.
- Give enough background to understand the present event.
- Use specific evidence to support statements about importance, scale, or consequence.
- Distinguish an exceptional example from a representative pattern.
- Do not use one anecdote as proof of a broad trend.
- Include relevant limitations in studies, polls, estimates, and data.
- State material assumptions and limitations.

## People and identification

- Identify a person by the information relevant to the story.
- Do not include race, ethnicity, religion, disability, age, gender, sexuality, appearance, or immigration status unless it is relevant.
- Ask people how they want to be identified when the distinction matters.
- Use a person's full name on first reference and surname on later references unless the publication requires another style.
- Avoid anonymous collective labels such as `critics say` or `experts believe` unless the group is identified and its relevance is explained.

## Quotations

- Quote a source exactly.
- Do not change a quotation's meaning through omission, punctuation, or surrounding context.
- Use a direct quotation when the exact words provide evidence, authority, character, or clarity.
- Paraphrase routine facts and background in simpler language.
- Do not use a quotation to state a basic fact that the writer can report directly.
- Keep quotations concise.
- Remove filler only when the publication's rules permit it and the meaning remains unchanged.
- Use ellipses and brackets sparingly.
- Do not combine fragments from different parts of an interview into a false continuous statement.
- Introduce a quotation with enough context to show why it matters.
- Put the source before a long quotation when readers must know who is speaking.
- Keep attribution close enough that readers can tell which statement it supports.

## Headlines, summaries, and captions

- State the central fact accurately.
- Use specific, concrete language and an active verb when possible.
- Keep the subject and action clear.
- Do not overstate what the article establishes.
- Do not use a question headline when the article can answer the question directly.
- Avoid puns, clichés, clickbait, and manufactured mystery.
- Do not imply causation, guilt, certainty, or consensus that the article does not support.
- Keep the headline, summary, caption, and article consistent.
- Use the summary to add important context instead of merely repeating the headline.

## Analysis and opinion

- Label analysis and opinion clearly.
- Keep reported facts distinct from the writer's interpretation.
- Support conclusions with evidence the reader can inspect or evaluate.
- State the material assumptions and limitations behind a conclusion.
- Do not use certainty as a substitute for evidence.
- Describe inferences as inferences.

## Editorial review checklist

Before publication, confirm that:

1. The lead states the central news or idea.
2. Every material fact is supported.
3. Each claim is attributed at the correct level.
4. The article distinguishes fact, uncertainty, analysis, and opinion.
5. The structure answers the reader's questions in a useful order.
6. Unfamiliar terms are explained.
7. Numbers include the context needed to understand them.
8. Quotations are exact and fairly represented.
9. People are identified accurately and only by relevant characteristics.
10. The headline does not overstate the article.
11. Material assumptions, disputes, and limitations are visible.
12. Every sentence earns its place.

# Overall standard

The finished text must be accurate, direct, readable, and suited to its purpose.

Technical writing must tell the reader exactly what is true, what to do, what limits apply, and what can happen.

Editorial writing must tell the reader what happened, how it is known, why it matters, and what remains uncertain.
